Thrive vs. YNAB: an honest comparison

July 31, 2026 · 6 min read · by Taylor Miller, who builds Thrive

I build one of these two apps, so read this with that in mind. I've tried to write the comparison I wanted when I was shopping, which means being straight about where YNAB is the better choice.

Both apps do zero-based budgeting: before the month starts, every bit of income is assigned a job, and the plan balances to zero. That method is the reason either app works. If you're deciding between them, you've already accepted the method — the real question is how each one handles your bank data, and what happens when you stop paying.

The short version

  Thrive YNAB
Free option Full budget, free forever 34-day trial, then paid
Paid price $8/mo for planning tools $14.99/mo, or $109/yr
Bank connection You export a file. No login. Direct import for many banks; file import otherwise
Where data lives Your devices, synced via your own iCloud YNAB's servers
Platforms Native Mac + iPhone Web, iOS, Android
Maturity New. Built by one person. Two decades, large team, huge community

Where YNAB is genuinely better

I'd rather say this first, because it's true and you'll find out anyway.

Direct bank import. YNAB pulls transactions automatically for many banks. Thrive doesn't, by design — you export a CSV, or let Thrive read the transaction alerts your bank already emails you. That's more setup on day one. If you want a budget that fills itself without you ever touching a file, YNAB does that and Thrive doesn't.

It runs anywhere. Windows, Android, a Chromebook, a friend's laptop. Thrive is Mac and iPhone only. If your household isn't all-Apple, this comparison is already over.

Twenty years of refinement and a real community. YNAB has taught its method to millions of people, with workshops, forums, and a body of advice built up over two decades. Thrive is new, and I'm one person. If you want a support forum at 11pm, they have one.

Where Thrive is different

A complete budget is free. Not a trial. Budgets, transactions, imports, the dashboard, the emergency fund, debt payoff — free, permanently. Premium is $8/month for the longer-horizon tools: retirement and college projections, real estate and mortgage payoff, the year-to-date review. Over a year that's $96 if you want those, and $0 if you don't, against $109 for YNAB.

No bank login, ever. This is the actual philosophical split. Most budgeting apps connect by having you enter your bank username and password, which are passed to a third-party data aggregator you never chose and have no relationship with. That company sees the transactions behind your login. How such firms handle and share consumer financial data has drawn class-action litigation and regulatory scrutiny in recent years. You can decide how comfortable that trade is — plenty of people are fine with it, and it does buy real convenience.

Thrive's answer is to not make the trade. You export a file yourself and hand it to an app running on your own machine. Your records sync between your Mac and iPhone through your iCloud account. There's no server of mine in the middle, because there's no server of mine at all. Go look at Thrive's App Store privacy label — it reads Data Not Collected, and Apple, not me, is the one enforcing that.

It's a real Mac app. Not a website in a window. Statement imports, retirement scenarios, mortgage payoff, and the year-to-date review are genuinely more pleasant on a large screen with a keyboard.

Giving comes first. Thrive treats giving as a first-priority category rather than whatever's left at month end. That's a conviction built into the product, and if it isn't yours, it's easy to ignore — but I'd rather tell you it's there.

Who should pick which

Choose YNAB if you want automatic bank sync above all, you're not on Apple hardware, or you want the reassurance of a long-established company and a big community.

Choose Thrive if you'd rather not hand your bank credentials to anyone, you're on a Mac and iPhone, you want a full budget without a subscription, or you want giving planned first.

They're both honest tools built around the same method. The disagreement is about what a budgeting app should be allowed to know about you.
