Why we built this

I got tired of being the product

July 31, 2026 · 4 min read · by Taylor Miller

I got tired of my data being somebody else's business model. Not just financial data — all of it. Somewhere along the way it became normal that using a piece of software means handing over a record of your life so a company can make money from it.

You notice it in small ways first. An ad for the thing you mentioned out loud. A recommendation that knows a little too much. Then one day you actually read what you agreed to, and the arrangement is right there in plain language: the product is free because you are what's being sold.

For most software that's annoying. For money software it's something else.

What a budgeting app actually knows

Your transaction history is the most revealing document you own. Not your email, not your photos — your spending. It knows where you were on Tuesday and who you were with. It knows what you're treating and which doctor you saw. It knows whether you're giving, gambling, job-hunting, or quietly struggling. It knows your marriage is in counseling before your family does.

Most budgeting apps get all of it. They connect to your accounts by asking for your bank username and password, then passing those credentials to a third-party data aggregator you never chose and have no relationship with. That company sees the transactions behind your login. How such firms handle and share consumer financial data has been the subject of class-action litigation and regulatory scrutiny in recent years.

Plenty of people are fine with that trade, and it does buy real convenience — transactions appear without you lifting a finger. I'm not here to tell anyone they're wrong for taking it.

I just didn't want to take it myself.

So I took matters into my own hands

My family was trying to do something ordinary and surprisingly hard: decide where our money should go before the month started, and actually know where it went once the month ended. Every tool that made that easy wanted the keys to everything. Every tool that didn't was a spreadsheet.

So I built the thing I wanted, on nights and weekends, for us. That's the whole origin story. Thrive exists because I wanted my own family's records to stay ours.

The rule we built it on is simple: we never see your data, because there is nowhere for it to go. There's no server of ours in the middle. Your records live on your Mac and iPhone and sync through your iCloud account. There's no bank login, ever — you export a file yourself and hand it to an app running on your own machine.

That's not a promise you have to take on faith. Look at Thrive's App Store privacy label. It reads Data Not Collected, and Apple — not us — is the one holding us to it.

Why the budget itself is free

If we believe people are being poorly served here, it would be strange to put the fix behind a paywall.

So a complete zero-based budget is free, permanently. Not a trial. Budgets, transactions, imports, the dashboard, the emergency fund, debt payoff — all free, and not crippled to push you somewhere. Your income gets assigned before the month starts, every category you care about gets funded on purpose, and at month end you can see exactly where it all went.

Premium is $8 a month, and it's for the longer horizon: retirement and college projections, real estate and mortgage payoff, the year-to-date review. Useful when you want it. Not needed to budget well. That's the whole business — a small subscription from people who want the planning tools, rather than a data pipe running quietly in the background.

What I'm asking

Try the free version. Export one CSV, drop it in, and see your month laid out honestly. It takes about fifteen minutes, and if it isn't for you, nothing follows you out the door — there's no account to delete, because there was never an account.
