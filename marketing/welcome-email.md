---

Hi — I'm Taylor.

I got tired of my data being somebody else's business model. Not just
financial data — all of it. Somewhere along the way it became normal that
using a piece of software means handing over a record of your life so a
company can make money from it.

Budgeting apps are a sharp version of that problem. Most of them connect to
your accounts by asking for your bank username and password, then passing
those credentials to a third-party data aggregator you never chose and have
no relationship with. That company sees the transactions behind your login.
How those firms have handled and shared consumer financial data has been the
subject of class-action litigation and regulatory scrutiny in recent years.
You can decide for yourself how comfortable that arrangement is.

I decided I wasn't, so I built the thing I wanted.

Thrive is a personal finance app for Mac and iPhone where your records
belong to you: they live on your own devices and sync through your own
private iCloud. There's no server of mine in the middle, and no bank login —
ever. You bring a file you exported yourself. That's the whole connection.

**A complete budget is free. Not a trial — free.**

Your income gets assigned before the month starts, giving comes first rather
than last, and at month end you can see exactly where it all went. Budgets,
transactions, imports, the dashboard, the emergency fund, debt payoff —
that's the free app, and it isn't hobbled to push you somewhere.

Premium ($8/month, with a week to try it) is for the longer horizon:
retirement and college projections, real estate and mortgage payoff, and the
year-to-date review. Worth it when you want it. Not needed to budget well.

**Your first budget takes about fifteen minutes**

**1. Export a CSV from your bank.** Log in, choose "Export" or "Download
transactions," pick CSV. One account is enough to start. That's the entire
connection — no password to hand over.

**2. Drop it into Thrive.** It reads the file and sorts the spending into
categories on the way in, so most of it is tagged before you look. You're
reviewing, not typing.

**3. Connect your email.** Let Thrive read the transaction alerts your bank
already sends you, and spending stays current all month instead of only when
you remember to export. Any IMAP inbox — Gmail, iCloud, Outlook, Yahoo,
Fastmail, AOL.

**4. Make the categories yours.** Rename, move, or add categories. Every
merchant you tag is remembered, so the same store sorts itself next time. A
few minutes here is what makes every later month automatic.

**5. Get your budget — and share it.** Thrive drafts a zero-based budget from
your real numbers, so your income is fully assigned and the month balances to
zero. Invite your spouse and you're both looking at the same plan — two Apple
IDs, no shared password.

## Legal wording — read before changing

The aggregator paragraph is written to be **defensible**:

- It describes a **mechanism** (credentials passed to a third party), which is
  how the technology works and is not in dispute.
- It says litigation and regulatory scrutiny **have occurred**, which is a
  matter of public record, without naming a company or asserting that any
  specific firm broke the law.
- It ends by letting the reader judge, rather than telling them a competitor
  is dishonest.

**Do not** name a specific aggregator alongside an accusation, and do not say
any company "sold your data" or "got caught." Naming a firm next to an
allegation is what turns marketing copy into a defamation risk. The paragraph
lands the point without needing to.

One factual note: **Stripe is a payments processor**, not a bank-data
aggregator — it isn't the company sitting behind budgeting-app bank
connections. Naming it would be inaccurate.

## Other wording

- Avoided the phrase "every dollar" throughout — it's a competitor's product
  name and not worth the collision.
